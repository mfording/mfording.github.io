Every Book I Read in 2025

with short reviews

For the past few years, I’ve set a goal of reading 50 books a year, which I’ve found is the sweet spot for me between not feeling motivated to read anything and feeling stressed about not reading enough. This year’s 50 books were pretty eclectic: classic fiction, contemporary fiction, poetry, romance, a lot of graphic novels, memoirs, and several mysteries (Tana French, I love you so much). Below is the list of all of my 2025 books, with brief reviews. Tell me what books you read in 2025!

[This list is also available as aposton my Substack.]

  1. The League of Extraordinary Gentlemen, Vol. I, Alan Moore:I love Kyle Kalgren’s video essay about this series, but I think cataloging all the literary references is more fun than actually reading it.
  2. Rejection, Tony Tulathimutte:one of the funniest, grossest, most incisive and insightful books I’ve ever read about young people and Internet culture. Read at your own peril (!!!); this also contains some of the most graphic and insane things I have ever read.
  3. The Sun Also Rises, Ernest Hemingway: nobody told me Hemingway was funny! A pleasant surprise. That said, this book is mostly boring, with occasional flashes of humor, violence, and pathos.
  4. The Witch Elm, Tana French:OOF. There are dream books and there are nightmare books, and this is a nightmare book. It horrifies and haunts me in a way that her other books never have.
  5. A Court of Frost and Starlight, Sarah J. Maas:I kind of love that she just decided to throw a little Christmas episode into the middle of this series. It’s short, it’s fun, the vibes are nicely wintry.
  6. The Long Way to a Small, Angry Planet, Becky Chambers:really liked this! I don’t read much sci-fi, so it was a fun change of pace. It’s a found-family adventure story with some genuinely thought-provoking ideas about how human and alien inter-species communication might work, if that ever came to be.
  7. Literary Hoaxes: An Eye-Opening History of Famous Frauds, Melissa Katsoulis:a collection of short descriptions of literary hoaxes, the style of this book is nothing to write (hah) home about, but some of the stories are interesting and entertaining. I read most of this in short bursts on the subway.
  8. Small Rain, Garth Greenwell:gorgeous. Greenwell is a beautiful prose stylist, and his latest work, set mostly in a hospital during the early days of the pandemic, is a quietly profound meditation on illness, mortality, and the ability of love to transform our fragile, finite lives.
  9. Bone, Vol. I: Out from Boneville, Jeff Smith:in March I was struck with a powerful nostalgia for the Bone series, which I read and loved in elementary school. It holds up! The characters and world are as vibrant and full of life as I remember.
  10. Foster, Claire Keegan:I can’t believe I hadn’t read any Claire Keegan before this year. This is a lovely, subtle novella about childhood and belonging, set in the Irish countryside, and Keegan’s style is mesmerizing.
  11. Fear of Flying, Erica Jong:staple of 1970s feminist literature. I liked it! I don’t really know what else to say about it, honestly. The heroine is fun.
  12. The Vegetarian, Han Kang:I don’t think I really…. get this book. But I appreciated it and thought it was quite disturbing and thought-provoking, in its best moments.
  13. Molly, Blake Butler:an incredibly painful, personal memoir of the author’s marriage to the poet Molly Brodak, her death, and the secrets he uncovered afterward. It’s a book that makes you believe in unconditional love, if only because there’s just no other explanation for how Butler was able to understand and forgive his wife after everything she put him through.
  14. Love and Other Poems, Alex Dimitrov:these poems are by turns silly and sad, lyrical and opaque. Dimitrov is relentlessly romantic and absolutely in love with New York City, a combination which rarely fails to win me over. The titular poem is an “endless” poem, an ongoing project.
  15. Things Fall Apart, Chinua Achebe:one of the best-known classics of African literature, I first heard about this novel while reading Heart of Darkness in high school English class. It’s a counter-narrative of the European colonization of Africa, from the point of view of an Igbo warrior facing the destruction of his traditions and community. Should be required reading for everyone.
  16. Expect More: Demanding Better Libraries for Today’s Complex World, R. David Lankes:when I was working in the cafe of my local public library as a teenager, I told the library director that I wanted to be a librarian, and he gave me this book. I reread it while I was beginning to work on my grad school applications, and I think its basic message—libraries are community centers, not just repositories of books—has only become more important since its publication in 2012.
  17. Spent, Alison Bechdel:Bechdel’s first graphic novel! It brings back several beloved characters from her iconic comic strip Dykes to Watch Out For, presented here as a close-knit group of middle-aged Vermonters navigating aging, family, and relationships both old and new. It’s very funny and charming, and I loved seeing these characters again.
  18. Abandon Me, Melissa Febos:I read this to pregame for her new book, which is a sort of sequel memoir to this one. It’s a a collection of essays about love and abandonment, and I thought it was fine, but nothing really special.
  19. Check, Please! Book 1, Ngozi Ukazu:This was a reread; I can’t remember when I first read this completely delightful graphic novel about a closeted figure skater/baker who joins his college hockey team, but it was just as good the second time.
  20. Howl and Other Poems, Allen Ginsberg:as part of my ongoing poetic education, I figured I should read some Beat poetry. Howl, is, obviously, iconic, but I have a special place in my heart for his poem about Whitman, “lonely old courage-teacher…”
  21. Exit Zero, Marie-Helene Bertino:I met Marie-Helene when I worked for the Kenyon Review, and she’s very nice. These short stories, most of them magical realism-inflected, are all witty and compassionate; my favorite is Viola in Midwinter, the final story.
  22. The Mysterious Affair at Styles, Agatha Christie:I maintain that the best beach read is a good mystery, and Agatha Christie is the best mystery writer, so I read this on vacation. This was her first detective novel, published in 1920, and introduces the inimitable Hercule Poirot.
  23. Daisy Miller, Henry James:pretty crazy that I managed to get through an entire English degree without ever being assigned Henry James. Daisy Miller is a classic, it’s extremely good, and I should read more James.
  24. The Searcher, Tana French:I think anyone who’s spoken to me in the last two years knows that I’ve been trying fervently to spread the gospel of Tana French. This is the first book in a duology, separate from her Dublin Murder Squad series, that centers on a jaded American cop who retires to a village in the west of Ireland and becomes a father figure to a neglected teenager whose brother has mysteriously disappeared. It’s insanely atmospheric—read it and feel instantly transported to the eerie, misty backroads of rural Ireland.
  25. Against Memoir, Michelle Tea:I became briefly, mildly obsessed with Michelle Tea after reading her memoir How to Grow Up in high school, but hadn’t picked up any of her other work until now. This book is a mix of personal essays and cultural criticism, and I liked most of it but wasn’t blown away.
  26. Boy Parts, Eliza Clark:Meh. I really am getting tired of the Young Woman Slowly Loses Her Mind genre and the “ooooh is this real or just all in her head?” thing. Plenty of books feature unreliable narrators much more compelling than this one. Just read Ottessa Moshfegh or Melissa Broder instead.
  27. Our Wives Under the Sea, Julia Armfield:very much a Book Where Nothing Happens, not necessarily in a bad way. I liked the atmosphere, but didn’t love the story as a whole.
  28. The Ornithologist’s Field Guide to Love, India Holton:two rival ornithologists in fantasy Edwardian England compete to capture a rare, magical bird and, of course, fall in love in the process. Silly and fun, not particularly memorable.
  29. Intermezzo, Sally Rooney:touching, precise, painfully specific about the ways in which people need each other—classic Rooney. I love these characters! My only complaint is that it’s WAY too long. Like, 150 pages too long.
  30. The Dry Season, Melissa Febos:a memoir of a year the author spent celibate as she interrogated her addictions to love and sex. It’s in the braided style, with personal accounts intertwined with musings on literature, history, and psychology; my favorite sections were about Sappho and the Beguines.
  31. Leaves of Grass, Walt Whitman:I fell in love with Whitman when I took a weekend class on him while at writing camp in Iowa as a teenager, but this is the first time I’ve read all of Leaves of Grass cover to cover. The poems just knock you off your feet—the force and scope of his vision was incredible, but so was its generosity, its deep and humane tenderness.
  32. The Hunter, Tana French:I can’t believe there’s no more Tana French for me to read. I want to go camp out outside her house until she publishes another book. This is a sequel to The Searcher, and I loved reading about those characters again, although I feel that this is a weaker story and wraps up much too neatly at the end.
  33. Long Bright River, Liz Moore:similar complaint as above: the ending wraps everything up too tidily. But even so, I liked it: this is a tense and propulsive literary thriller that centers on a cop investigating a string of murders in Philadelphia amid the opioid crisis. The prose is really, really good.
  34. Perfection, Vincenzo Latronico:exactly as its title promises, this is a perfect book. I’m obsessed with it. It’s about an aimless millennial expat couple in Berlin, and the internet, and coveting a light-filled apartment with beautiful hardwood floors. Everyone under the age of 40 should read it.
  35. We Ride Upon Sticks, Quan Barry:this was my pick for the book club I have with some of my friends from volunteering. It’s completely bizarre, and felt like it kept building to a climax that never actually happened, but I like it because it’s so unique.
  36. I Who Have Never Known Men, Jacqueline Harpman:an underground (hah) feminist classic from the 90s, this book blew up on TikTok last year and eventually, I heard about it. It’s one of the few BookTok darlings that deserves the attention. Deeply sad, deeply disquieting.
  37. The Chronology of Water, Lidia Yuknavitch:I reread this because I heard that the movie, directed by Kristen Stewart, is coming out soon. It was really exciting when I read it for the first time at 19, but I think it’s lost some of its power as I’ve gotten older/everyone else has started writing experimental memoirs. Still great, though.
  38. The Passengers, John Marrs:also read this for book club. It’s awful! Don’t read it!
  39. The Song of the Lark, Willa Cather:pure and perfect, this is the story of a girl from a rural Colorado town in the 1890s who, through sheer force of will, transforms herself into a great artist. Thea Kronborg forever.
  40. I Love Dick, Chris Krause:I got weird looks reading this on the subway. Can’t imagine why. It’s a 90s cult classic blend of memoir and feminist theory, recounting the author’s psychosexual obsession with the titular Dick, her husband’s colleague. I enjoyed it about half of the time; the theory mostly bored me.
  41. Big Fan, Alexandra Romanoff:short, efficient, and satisfying, this is a cute romance about a political strategist and the former boy-band member who recruits her to help launch his solo career. It’s definitely inspired by One Direction, but I don’t know enough about One Direction to figure out which member this guy is supposed to be.
  42. Couplets, Maggie Milner:I had high hopes for this one and was deeply disappointed. I like rhyming couplets as much as the next English major, but if you’re writing a narrative in them, is it too much to ask that all of the couplets actually rhyme? There’s some clever language and interesting lines here, but overall it’s quite boring.
  43. The Amazing Adventures of Kavalier and Clay, Michael Chabon:you really fall in love with these characters, which is good because you spend a LONG time with them (over 700 pages). Funny, touching, worthy of its status as a modern American classic.
  44. Crush, Richard Siken:If you were ever on tumblr.com, you have read a Richard Siken poem. It’s hard to believe I hadn’t read this entire collection, his debut, until now. His work is brilliant, ruthlessly disciplined, often funny, and more often, completely heartbreaking.
  45. A Lost Lady, Willa Cather:every new Willa Cather book I read is my new favorite Willa Cather book. The characters in this are so finely drawn, you can almost hear them breathe.
  46. Persuasion, Jane Austen:A reread of my favorite Austen novel, because Emily and I visited Bath while I was in England. It’s perfect.
  47. Small Things Like These, Claire Keegan:I actually watched the movie with Cillian Murphy before I read this—the movie is great, and a very faithful adaptation, but the book is better. It’s a short novella about a quiet family man faced with a moral choice in 1980s rural Ireland, and it’s beautiful and profound.
  48. Check, Please! Book 2: Sticks and Scones, Ngozi Ukazu:the second half of the Check, Please! webcomic, it’s a satisfying conclusion to the story; fairly unrealistic, but I don’t really want realism from my wholesome college hockey romances, so all’s well.
  49. Nimona, N. D. Stevenson:I think this graphic novel was made into a Netflix movie pretty recently? I wasn’t completely sold on it at the start, but by the end, I was completely won over. Great artwork, great storytelling.
  50. Kitchen, Banana Yoshimoto:a sweet, sad, heartfelt slice-of-life story about two grieving people and the deep bond they form. The book includes Kitchen as well as a companion story, “Moonlight Shadow,” which can be described in much the same way. Thanks for the rec, Molly!
