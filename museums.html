Museum Studies

Below are some selected reviews I’ve written on museum exhibits over the past few years.

Superfine, Metropolitan Museum of Art

[From June 2025]

In an effort to make the most of my absolute best work perk—the ability to get into almost any museum in the city for free—every month I’ll be doing a little review of a museum exhibit.

This month’s exhibit is "Superfine: Tailoring Black Style," currently on view at the Met. If you watched any of the coverage of this year’s Met Gala, you’ll have some familiarity with the exhibit, because it served as the inspiration for the gala’s dress code, “Tailored for You.”

I think this is one of the best exhibits I’ve ever seen. The space itself is gorgeously designed, with black walls and floors and dramatic lighting illuminating mannequins posed both on platforms rising from the floor and floating shelves lining the walls. The music playing softly throughout the space feels perfect for the feel of the show (I have no idea what it was, but it sounded great). As you walk through the exhibit, it’s organized along a loose chronology, beginning with examinations of Black style and the beginning of “dandyism” in Europe and America in the 1600s-1700s, but it’s also organized by 12 sections with titles such as “Heritage” and “Disguise.”

There’s a wonderful mix of historical material, including extant garments, with corresponding designs by contemporary artists. Everywhere you turn, there’s an object that surprises or arrests you: one of Frederick Douglass’ shirts, with an embroidered “D”; a belt designed to look like hands clasping a bouquet of golden flowers; André Leon Talley’s vintage Louis Vuitton luggage; a first edition of Olaudah Equiano’s 1789 memoir. The exhibit’s title is taken from a line in that book, where Equiano describes purchasing his freedom from enslavement. He writes that he also “laid out about eight pounds of my money for a suit of superfine clothes to dance with at my freedom."

It’s an exhibit that feels grand and sweeping while simultaneously remaining grounded in its goal: what the Met refers to as demonstrating “how one’s self-presentation is a mode of distinction and resistance—within a society impacted by race, gender, class, and sexuality.” Emily loves clothes and knows a lot about them; I definitely do not. But we both agreed that the exhibit has the effect of convincing you, completely, of how much the act of clothing oneself matters, how essential it is in the lifelong process of self-fashioning.

A Lively Mind, The Morgan Library

[From July 2025]

The exhibit I’m reviewing this month is "A Lively Mind: Jane Austen at 250," currently on view at the Morgan Library until September 14th. Anyone who has spent more than ten minutes talking to me knows that I love Jane Austen/worship the memory of Jane Austen/have read everything Jane Austen ever wrote (including juvenilia and unpublished fragments), so I did feel obligated to attend. Plus, I love the Morgan. They have the best museum gift shop ever, and at closing time they manually ring a huge vintage bell from J. P. Morgan’s yacht, and everybody has to flee the building immediately because it’s so loud.

The Austen exhibit is on the first floor, and it’s fairly modest in size—you can take your time and still be done in less than an hour, even if you’re like me and have a compulsive need to read every single piece of wall text. The design is simple and unobtrusive, with deep green walls and accents of gold and cream. An internal dividing wall features built-in windows and wallpaper based on original designs from Chawton Cottage in Hampshire, England, where Austen lived with her mother and sister/bestie, Cassandra. The first two-thirds of the exhibit focus on Austen’s life and her writings, while the final third is devoted to her posthumous reception in North America and Austen collectors, especially Augusta Burke, who donated several manuscripts to the Morgan upon her death in 1975.

The first section has most of the interesting items, with the best being the only surviving complete manuscript of any of Austen’s works: the novella Lady Susan, my absolute favorite. They’ve also got a trove of Jane’s letters to Cassandra (one of which has cross-hatched writing in order to save paper, since postage was paid by the letter receiver in that era). Most of the objects in the exhibit are text-based (letters, manuscripts, first editions of the novels, etc.) but there are a few flashier items, such as a reconstruction of a pelisse that Austen wore and a simple turquoise ring, an Austen family heirloom, that was briefly owned by Kelly Clarkson in 2012. At the end of the first section, an overhead projector beams the text of Austen’s tombstone onto the floor, which I think is the only aesthetic misstep of the exhibit—it just looks tacky.

The second section is a little dull, but I understand that the curators were probably required to pay homage to the collectors whose donations made this exhibit possible. There are a couple of interesting touches, though, such as a lock of Austen’s hair (strawberry blonde, though it may have faded with time) and a portrait of a young man by Amy Sherald, fittingly titled A Single Man in Possession of a Good Fortune. I also liked an array of posthumous portraits of Austen, which illustrated how her actual physical appearance, unfamiliar to the public during her life, bears very little resemblance to the popular image of her that we know today.

The curators of this exhibit have used this collection of objects to make Austen in the twenty-first century feel both reassuringly cozy and excitingly fresh, a difficult feat to pull off. There’s the Jane everybody knows and loves here; the letters and historical material emphasize her powerful intellect, wry humor, and deep engagement with pop culture, fashion, and literature, the evidence of which appears in her novels. But by displaying her personal financial accounts, along with her detailed notes on the reception of her fiction manuscripts among her friends and family, the exhibit pushes back on a popular trope about Austen: that she wrote only for the amusement of her immediate circle, and didn’t care about fame or money. But, like most writers of the past and present, she did care—she wanted her books to sell well, and she wanted readers to love them. After her death in 1817, her family, following the conventions of the time, presented an image of her as a proper, demure gentlewoman: her brother insisted she never wanted literary recognition, and Cassandra censored some of her letters by cutting out lines deemed too rude or unladylike.

Despite these efforts, walking through this exhibit makes you feel the presence of Austen’s singular voice, by turns warm, witty, biting, fond, cynical, and amused. In a letter to her niece following Austen’s death, Cassandra lamented that she had lost “such a friend as never can have been surpassed,—She was the sun of my life, the gilder of every pleasure, the soother of every sorrow...” The Morgan’s exhibit has the rare power of painting a picture of Jane Austen so vivid, so alive, that you can begin to see the remarkable person Cassandra was grieving.

Rashid Johnson: a Poem for Deep Thinkers, Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum

[From September 2025]

For some reason, I have never been to the Guggenheim before now, even though it’s only a 20-minute walk from my apartment. My inaugural visit was to see the exhibit Rashid Johnson: A Poem for Deep Thinkers, a mid-career retrospective for Johnson.

The exhibit starts at the bottom of the Guggenheim’s internal staircase/exhibition space, a quarter-mile-long white ramp that spirals upward toward a massive skylight. In the center of the space, plants with burlap-wrapped roots hang from the ceiling, swaying slightly. The pieces are arranged in chronological order, beginning with an early work from 2006 and ascending toward his most recent work, Sanguine, a site-specific installation at the very top of the rotunda. As you walk upward, you pass by sculptures, paintings, photographs, and video installations, giving you a sense of the scope of Johnson’s work over the years and how much his style has evolved. He has a consistent set of materials and motifs that are repeated across many of his pieces: black soap, shea butter, books, plants, glass, crosshair targets, red ovals. The ovals are meant to invoke the image overlaid on your vision after looking directly at the sun, an action invoked in the poem from which the exhibit takes its name, “A Poem for Deep Thinkers” by Amiri Baraka. There’s also a recognizable sense of humor in several of his works, the funniest being a pallet of shrink-wrapped books on the floor entitled Contemporary Black Male Literature Starter Kit.

My favorite piece in the exhibit was Sanguine, which consists of several large rows of shelves full of potted plants and books (The Black Interior by Elizabeth Alexander, The Stranger by Albert Camus, and The Crisis of the Negro Intellectual by Harold Cruse, which shows up in multiple pieces throughout the exhibit), a piano, and a short film. In the film, Johnson, a child, and an older man form a multigenerational family who pick flowers, grill meat, walk along a beach, and hold up African masks in front of their faces. The red ovals make a reappearance, and there are shots of Johnson making some of the pieces shown in the exhibit, including burning his crosshair motif into a wooden board and breaking apart clods of shea butter. There’s no dialogue, and the film feels like a fitting and elegant culmination of the themes and ideas of Johnson’s work as a whole, as the viewer sits and watches it after their journey up that huge, winding staircase.

The abundance of plants in Johnson’s work, especially in Sanguine, reminded me of Hanif Abdurraqib’s poem cycle “How Can Black People Write About Flowers at a Time Like This;” one of the poems is linked here. Abdurraqib (one of my favorite contemporary poets/essayists) said about the poems:

“I was at a reading shortly after the election, and the poet (who was black) was reading gorgeous poems, which had some consistent and exciting flower imagery. A woman (who was white) behind me—who thought she was whispering to her neighbor—said ‘How can black people write about flowers at a time like this?’ I thought it was so absurd in a way that didn’t make me angry but made me curious. What is the black poet to be writing about ‘at a time like this’ if not to dissect the attractiveness of a flower—that which can arrive beautiful and then slowly die right before our eyes? I thought flowers were the exact thing to write about at a time like this, so I began this series of poems, all with the same title. I thought it was much better to grasp a handful of different flowers, put them in a glass box, and see how many angles I could find in our shared eventual demise.”

Johnson’s plants also evoke the fragility of the human body, particularly the Black body, but they also gesture toward the hope and tenderness inherent in tending to living things: the pots the plants sit in are often handmade by Johnson, and the scenes of multigenerational family life in Sanguine feature flowers from a lush, green garden.
